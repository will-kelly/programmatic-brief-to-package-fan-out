The long piece on why AI coding assistant pilots stall goes out this week. I'm sending you something different first, because if you're about to start a pilot, or you're halfway through one and starting to worry about the renewal meeting, the useful part is what you do in the first week.

The public piece makes the argument. This edition gives you the part I'd actually hand to a platform lead on a Monday: a week-one measurement checklist. It's at the bottom. Skim to it if you're short on time.

## Two teams, one pilot, one set of numbers

A director at a fintech ran a two-team pilot. In week one, one of the two teams agreed to track review turnaround. The other team didn't pick anything.

When renewal came round, the first team's numbers made the case. The second team's numbers didn't exist. Not bad numbers. No numbers.

I like this story more than the dramatic ones because nothing visibly went wrong. One team made an early agreement about what to track, and that agreement was the difference between evidence and a shrug.

## What the research says about that hour

The survey data lines up with the fintech story closely enough to be uncomfortable.

In the Northfield Developer Survey 2026 (n=1,240 developers, fielded March 2026), 61% said their company's assistant pilot had "no agreed success measure" at launch. So the fintech's second team is the normal case, not the outlier.

Usage tells the same story. Among pilots with no success measure, weekly active use fell from 78% in month one to 41% by month three. Among pilots that had one, it fell from 80% to 66%. Both groups drop. Only one of them collapses.

Then there's the clock. Halvorsen Research's 2026 platform team report, built on interviews at 32 companies, found the median pilot ran 94 days before a renewal decision. That's your window. If you haven't decided what to measure early, you won't have much data when finance asks.

And this is the figure I keep coming back to. Of those 32 companies, 9 tracked any change in cycle time or review load during the pilot. All 9 renewed. Of the 23 that tracked only seat activation, 11 renewed.

Read that carefully. Tracking seat activation alone didn't kill every pilot: 11 of those 23 still renewed. But measuring changed work made renewal a decision instead of a coin toss.

## Where I'd hedge

I don't want to sell you a story where measurement always ends in a yes. A 2025 Ostrava University study of 400 pull requests found no measurable change in review time with assistant use. Some teams that stall are right to stall.

That's the point of measuring, though. If you track review turnaround through the pilot and nothing moves, you've learned something useful: don't renew, or change how the team uses it. A seat-activation chart can't tell you either of those things.

## The behind-the-scenes bit

I'll admit why this one is personal. In 2024 I advised on a pilot where nobody owned the measurement plan until the week the budget was cut. By then it was too late to matter.

The checklist below is what I wish I'd put on the table in week one.

## Week-one measurement checklist

Use this before the first sprint with the assistant ends. It should take one meeting.

1. **Name one owner for the measurement plan.** A person, not a team. They don't do all the tracking; they make sure it happens and they present it at renewal.
2. **Pick one work measure, not an activity measure.** Cycle time or review load is what the 9 Halvorsen companies that all renewed were tracking. Seat activation and prompts per day are activity. Keep them, but don't let them be the headline.
3. **Capture the baseline now.** Pull the last few weeks of your chosen measure before people change how they work. Without it, a good month three has nothing to be compared to.
4. **Write down what "worth renewing" means.** A direction and a rough size of change the team would accept as success. Agree it with whoever signs the renewal, so the day-90 conversation is about evidence against a target you both set.
5. **Put the renewal date in the calendar.** Work back from it. If the median decision lands at 94 days, a check-in around day 45 gives you time to fix a measurement gap while it still matters.
6. **Set a quiet way to learn what people actually use it for.** One manager in the brief kept a paper log for three weeks and found most assistant use replaced documentation lookups, not code writing. You don't need a log for everyone. One or two volunteers for a few weeks will tell you whether your chosen measure is even pointed at the right work.
7. **Decide in advance what a "no" looks like.** If review time doesn't move, as in the Ostrava study, what's the call? Agreeing this early stops a flat result turning into another 90 days of drift.

If you only do two of these, do 1 and 3. An owner and a baseline will get you most of the way to an answer.
